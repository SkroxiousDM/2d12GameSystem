# GRENDEL — Consolidated Master Rulebook

Apocalyptic adventures in a fantasy Iron Age

Editorial consolidation of seven supplied Grendel documents. Grendel terminology is retained; no Hollow Frontier terminology or outside-system rules have been introduced.

## Editorial policy and how to use this book

This is an editorial consolidation draft, not a newly authored rules edition. The main chapters combine the core procedures with the updated 2024 player material. Duplicate versions are not presented as simultaneous rules. Earlier-only options and materially different versions are distinguished in the appendices.

Precedence used for this compilation:

1. The 12 June 2024 player document takes precedence on subjects it explicitly covers.
2. The dated 2021 supplements take precedence over older versions of the same subject when the 2024 document does not replace them.
3. Core Rules v1.0 supplies the underlying resolution, conflict, advancement, recovery, seasonal and campaign procedures where no later replacement is supplied.
4. Conflicts within a single document, missing supporting procedures, and consequential ambiguities remain flagged. An editorial preference is not represented as an authorial ruling.

Terminology has been harmonised in retained core procedures to the 2024 Grendel vocabulary: Fighter → Warrior; Runecaster → Runespeaker; Instinct/Instincts → Cunning; Connections → Kith & Kin; Recovery Phase → Community Phase. These are editorial compatibility mappings between Grendel versions, not proof that every associated mechanic was unchanged. Original terms remain in historical notes and source extracts.

Source markers in HTML comments record PDF page numbers, including a cover or contents page. Printed page numbers may differ: the 2024 PDF's printed page number is generally one less than its PDF page number. Original in-text page references are source-local, not page numbers for this Markdown file. Some were already incorrect or unfinished in the PDFs; use this file's linked contents instead.

Read the unresolved-rules register before play. In particular, the 2024 player document refers to supporting systems not fully explained in the supplied core edition. No missing costs, acquisition rates, stat blocks or host conversions have been invented.

## 1. The world of Grendel

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 3. -->

To whoever may find this,

As one of our few surviving who knows your letters, I feel duty-bound to write this account, which I attest with my seal and my name. We owe this record to any others who may yet still be drawing breath, whoever they are and wherever they may be, and also to those yet to come, if the gods will.

Although I lay upon my deathbed, growing weaker each day from a cut gone bad, know that these are not the ravings of an old madman succumbing to fever. My vision is clear enough to see that my hall will surely fall to the darkness in the coming weeks, and with it my name and my line. It is that clarity which compels me now to write this account.

I have been fortunate enough in my life to serve as an aide to two kings, and both were just and fair. I know that few advisors to monarchs can say as much, and I do not carry my good fortune lightly. Like his father who bore his name before him, King Hraelgar had been valorous and self-sacrificing, and his decision to sail north came as no surprise.

Fishers and sailors had brought word to Korrigsford of a growing Iverni war fleet, mustering off the coast to the northwest. Relationships between our peoples had been tense for years, and although our merchants would often trade for the abundant Ivernian peat and shale, it was just as likely for deals to be negotiated through axe and shield as with the coin purse. Although raids and skirmishes were common, the type of full-scale war that a fleet of this size would portend had not been seen for a lifetime.

Upon hearing news of the fleet, King Hraelgar immediately sent word to his vassals to sound the horns of war. The late king inspired such loyalty and bravery among his jarls that his call received a swift and full-throated response, and a mighty army assembled within weeks. King Hraelgar vowed to crush the Iverni at sea, or to slay them in their halls if they fled. The King’s war fleet set off north for Ivernia, in the

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 4. -->

summer of 449 of the Domitian calendar, under the sound of horns and the singing of glorious tales.

The accounts of what happened next are almost impossible to believe. Had I not heard them from men I know to be of good standing and character, I would not have believed them either. As horrifying as these tales are, the next ten years would prove that even worse lay in store for our people.

The first scouts who set eyes on the coastline of Ivernia found no war fleet amassing, but something more alarming still. Barely a league from the white cliffs of Haver, the King’s scouting party came across the derelict, creaking remains of a shattered fleet. The scout who gave me his account of that day recalled the overwhelming noise of the sunken fleet, as the half-drowned survivors struggled to make their cries for mercy heard over the shrieking of seabirds plucking at bloated corpses.

We would learn too late that the Iverni had not gathered to storm our beaches, but rather to escape an even more dreadful foe. Their escape had proven unsuccessful.

King Hraelgar ordered the survivors to be brought aboard his fleet, but struggled to make sense of their accounts. Their tales were the ravings of the mad, or the ecstatic visions of druids, or the bedtime horrors of children. They spoke of an all-consuming darkness, blood-red skies, nightmares given flesh and serpents rising from the deep. Each one of the survivors, if accounts of the King’s men are to be believed, was overwhelmed with grief and terror.

The King struggled to make sense of what he’d heard, to comprehend what had happened, but he knew that nothing good could come of it. He ordered his fleets ashore and his armies to make camp by their boats for the night, returning home with the Ivernian survivors at first dawn. Gods be thanked that the dawn ever arrived for King Hraelgar.

In the early morning, before the break of first light, the King’s army was woken by the desperate sounding of horns, the shrieking of warning calls, and the clanging of weapons. Those who shook the sleep from their eyes may have believed they were still in a dream, as they saw the corpses of the Iverni sailors — scores of them, bloated and purple from the seawater — lurching ashore and assaulting the night’s picket with flotsam and waterlogged weapons.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 5. -->

Soldiers frantically took up arms and rushed to form a defence, but the sheer numbers of the dead meant that they would surely be overwhelmed. King Hraelgar led a disciplined, fighting retreat to secure the longboats and set sail, amid a desperate clawing and thrashing as throngs of dead men clambered up their hulls.

Countless men and boats were lost during the retreat, but this was a mere taste of what would soon follow. As King Hraelgar’s fleet sailed from Ostilla, hacking at the dead men as they went, they saw a grim sight on the horizon. An enormous fleet of black longboats with black sails, crewed by dark, hulking figures, gradually crawled towards them from the west.

As this fleet grew closer, the King’s sailors took better stock of their new foes. Standing a full head taller than a man, hunched and hulking, covered in thick, dark fur, they looked like creatures from a children’s tale. And so we named them trolls, and so on that day we tasted their fury. The King’s fleet could not outrun the black vessels in pursuit, so turned to meet them head on. Prow met prow and axe met shield, as fierce fighting erupted on the water.

It was during this clash that King Hraelgar, last king of our people, would fall. When his longboat met a hulking black vessel on the water, helmed by an enormous troll jarl standing eight feet and covered in a mass of bristles, the King rushed to answer its roaring challenge. Although King Hraelgar fought valiantly, he was struck down by the enormous hammer of the bellowing beast, and thus his line would end heirless, in sorrow and in glory.

King Hraelgar was not the only one to fall that day, nor since. The shattered remains of his fleet fled to land in Singsby, with the victors in swift pursuit, and upon landfall made haste for the King’s ancestral seat of Korrigsford. The northwest coast of our realm was swallowed by the enemy, as peasants, fishers and landholders alike uprooted their lives and fled towards the capital.

Upon hearing news of her husband’s loss, Queen Sigrid stood resolute. Despite the immensity of her grief, she knew that she must stand firm for her people. She rallied the survivors of the late King’s army, who together with her own personal house troops hastily prepared Korrigsford for a siege.

Barely two days passed between news of the King’s death and the arrival of our foe. I remember it well, the eeriness of it all. There were no banners massing on the

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 6. -->

horizon, no horns of war, just a descending chill. The night turned unseasonably dark, and then in mid-autumn it began to snow. What began as a light dusting of frost grew within the hour to a howling blizzard, despite the day earlier being sunny and cloudless.

We stood there, huddled watching, chilled to our bones. But then, just as the cold was almost enough to drive us to our knees, they were upon us. I may be an old man, but I was not far from the battleline that night, not far from the Queen’s side. Our watch was interrupted by a hew and cry some yards away, and we all turned. A panic broke out within the ranks, as somewhere along the line the palisades were breached and a horde of snarling trolls were in our midst. I cannot describe the ferocity of them. Steel rang out and clashed with steel as men were torn asunder, by the trolls wicked blades or even their bare claws. Soon we were engaged directly. The Queen’s personal guard stood firm, even as one fell after another, and they quickly became overwhelmed. In all my raids and battles I had never feared more for my life.

Then we saw it, racing towards us. A gargantuan specimen, a full head-and-shoulders taller than the others and garbed in a dark sash or cloth of some kind, wearing a mask of iron. It was carrying an enormous axe, chagrin directly towards the Queen. Before the beast could reach us, however, something indescribable happened. One of the Queen’s retinue, a blue-daubed priestess from Dormer named Aggni who served as her advisor, strode towards the creature and cast a handful of runestones upon the ground. The crone uttered an ancient tone, and from the stones sprang a strange sunlight which bound the beast. The Queen took this moment to stride decisively towards the creature, spear in hand, and impale it through the heart.

Seeing what must have been their king or general topple to the ground caused confusion among the beasts, which gave us our opportunity to escape. I wish I could describe this as a disciplined retreat, but I have no shame in admitting that we broke and ran. We fled, along with whatever other survivors had sense, and abandoned the late King’s ancestral home.

Two grim memories remain etched in my mind of the flight: the walls and halls of Korrigsord burning in the chilled, gloomy sky, and the figure silhouetted against the flames. Moving among the trolls without fear was a shadowy figure, man-sized, but graceful and supple as a dancing maiden or a nimble young warrior. I know that, whatever it was, it looked upon me as I fled. The mere thought of that creature is enough to chill me.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 7. -->

We withdrew south to Elskinborg, the Queen’s ancestral home and arguably the best-defended fortress in the realm. It’s seven-tired earthworks and double palisade gave us some small comfort on our arrival, but we knew that respite would be short-lived and that siege preparations must soon commence. We readied garrisons, gave every able-bodied man and woman we could find a weapon, and hastily filled the cellars with roots and grain to try to at least outlast our foe.

Once the preparations were done we waited. And we waited. Week after interminable week passed with sight nor sound of our enemy, so we reluctantly sent scouts north. They returned within the month with six score of refugees from Yarrow Hill, who brought with them a strange tale. Their leader, an odd priest named Thedric, explained that the enemy had marched east to capture their unimportant hamlet rather than pursuing our surviving forces south. At the time I considered it a tactical error, but now I’m not so certain.

The scouts also brought other news, the woeful tale of the villages and towns left behind in the north. We learned that their soil had become salt, and their trees withered. None of their streets were littered with dead, despite the dreadful ruin wreaked on hall and hovel alike, it was as though their people had simply vanished. The scouts explained that they could not get closer to Korrigsford or Yarrow Hill, as they were repelled by an unseasonably harsh and bitter snow accompanied by a dreadful, otherworldly howl.

The months without further attacks turned into a year, and our people began returning to the lives they had known before. Although we reckoned with the losses of the previous year with stiff-jawed determination, it was our next discovery which nearly broke us. The messengers we sent to the neighbouring land of Celtira to the south were never heard from again, while those we sent east to Esset delivered the same sorry tale that our scouts had brought from Korrigsford: that where once was a kingdom there now stood a wasteland. It appeared as though our neighbours were all defeated, and that our people were all that remained against the darkness. A darkness which loomed always on the horizon, in every direction.

Queen Sigrid’s brief moment of triumph over the troll king, along with that of her witch Aggni, brought us some faint hope in these dark times. It was a hope that the ancient knowledge of crones and prophets might allow us to bind and defeat the enemies we know will surely come, if not now then soon. Those who knew runes and letters began to search old pages and tablets for some new knowledge, druids came down from their mountain homes in the west to describe their beliefs, and even

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 8. -->

young children began to learn the ancient language of runes which was all but forgotten, even in my grandfather’s grandfather’s time. The insular, strange folk of Halskirk Isle, famed for its standing runestones twice the height of a man, even set about crafting further stones at the edges of our lands as a ward against our foes.

As a man who spent his youth under sail with axe in hand, it reassures me to write that superstition and religion have not been our only shield against the darkness. Queen Sigrid has expanded her personal guard to protect not just her person and holdings, but her entire realm. This new retinue of house guard, the Huscarls, requires more than simply mettle on the battlefield to meet the challenges of this new and dreadful age.

The Huscarls recruit specialists in all areas: warriors yes, but also trackers, lorekeepers, priests and druids, blacksmiths, hunters, sailors, apothecaries. Any who might be of value and who are willing to pledge an undying oath to not only the Queen’s service, but to the defence of the realm and the people. They are responsible for keeping Queen Sigrid’s peace, spreading messages through the realm, patrolling our border, dealing with traitors and brigands, and defending us from the outlanders who prowl through the darkness. Sometimes the Huscarls even plunge themselves into the lands north and east which are now ash and frost, to recover some ancient relic or forgotten lore.

Sadly, even the protections offered by priests and Huscarls were not enough to ward against the loss of more lands, and more people, in the coming years. The fall of Skelford and Esterholm has the heaviest blow, in the winter of the year 454 by your keeping. We learned about it from the throngs of starved, frostbitten survivors who had trudged through the cold from the east, bringing feverish tales of a monstrous wurm which had ruined their lands. We had seen enough by then not to doubt them. A handful of other communities have since fallen, each one with countless tales of sorrow to sing. Even the smallest of our holdings, a tiny fishing village named Atverness to the southwest, simply vanished three winters ago, a bleak reminder that some day all our time will come.

Although several years have passed since the loss of these communities, the terrors of the dark remain unrelenting, and our people are anxious. Even the smallest of our villages now contain some manner of fortification, to stave off even temporarily the horrors which prowl our lands. Huscarls, sailors and hunters report regular sightings of trolls lumbering through the forests, and dead men rising from their barrows. It is

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 9. -->

not uncommon for fishing boats to be lost at sea, or for journeyers to venture into the forests and never emerge, falling prey to the perils of this new age.

More alarming though than even the foul creatures stalking our lands are the lands themselves. Those living closest to the border report that their fields have grown sour, lakes gone rancid, herds of livestock succumbed to the pox and streams choked with dead fish. The trees of the forest twist and wither, beasts and birds turn rabid and wild, and valuable ore crumbles to dust. This malaise continues to slowly seep inward, and I fear that in time it will afflict our entire realm.

Although a lesser folk may have buckled under these hardships, the horrors of this new age have made us resolute, determined to resist and defy the darkness which surrounds us. The common folk have made many sacrifices to ensure the success of the war effort, which is how we now refer to the grim act of survival. I admire their fortitude: none have uttered complaint as they’ve gone without the holy days and feasts which filled earlier years. Today’s youths, both rich and poor, often live without even simple pleasures like dolls and trinkets. Any scrap of ore or timber can find a home, can find a use somewhere in our survival, and is rarely wasted on trivial things.

As only to be expected of one so magnanimous, the Queen has also gone without any form of luxury or finery, living a life her forebears would have seen as harsh and austere. Queen Sigrid has gone so far as to sacrifice her ancestral tapestries to be respun into blankets, and to strip the iron from ornaments and sigils in her hall to be melted down into blades and horseshoes. The Queen, like her people, stands resolute, and in her they place their trust.

Despite the darkness which seems to have no end to its hunger, Queen Sigrid has been a beacon of hope. Many feel that it is her will and faith alone which holds us together. She rules with grace, much like her husband before her, and without her firm yet even hand I expect we would succumb to bickering and infighting. I beseech the gods for her ongoing health, and am thankful for her continued radiance. Some have expressed concern that she has not yet chosen an heir, nor does she have any living children, but thankfully her strength and radiance have endured. These may be the sentimental ravings of an old fool, but in my eyes she looks not a day older than when the throne was thrust upon her, a widow too young, those ten years ago.

And so we persist, knowing that the darkness lays not far beyond our towns and villages, and that someday it will surely descend. Even on better days, when we can see the sun, the horizons are always as dark and ominous as an approaching storm.

In summer the coming of nightfall can send a chill through the most hardened warrior, with the fear that the sun may not rise again. The long nights of winter would drive some to grief, but to give in to despair would be an insult to our ancestors. We endure, we live, and we spit in the face of whoever, whatever would deny us that.

I hope that whoever finds this letter lives in an age of feasts and abundance, and will forgive me the indulgence of our woeful song. I lastly urge that you do not dismiss this account of our sorrows as a mere tale. If this darkness ever returns to the land I wish you the fortune to achieve what I could not.

Eyvind, son of Eyvind, Jarl of Marieborg and loyal servant to Queen Sigrid The year 461 of the Domitian calendar

## 2. Playing Grendel

Grendel is a narrative-driven, cooperative, mission-based roleplaying game. Characters are Queen Sigrid’s Huscarls and their companions, defending the realm against an encroaching supernatural catastrophe. Scarcity, grim survival and defiance are central to its tone.

The group resolves disputed interpretations by consensus. The GM frames obstacles and consequences, but should not simply overrule the table. A successful player narrates the outcome of their character's action, consistent with the result.

A session normally follows mission briefing, the Adventure Phase, the Community Phase, and session conclusion. The core recommends approximately two to four hours.

### Dice notation

- D: a six-sided die added to a pool; +1D means one extra die.
- S: a success; +1S means an added success where the rule permits it.
- Ob: obstacle, the difficulty of an independent test.
- Rank/rating: a character's recorded value in a skill, knowledge, trait or attribute.

### Character measures

The five attributes in the 2024 vocabulary are Cunning, Vigour, Will, Resources, and Kith & Kin. In the retained core framework, personal attributes range from 1–7; Resources and Kith & Kin range from 1–10. Skills and knowledges range from 2–7 once learned; traits range from 1–3. Starting caps are specified in character creation.

Cunning covers reactions, awareness and self-preservation; Vigour covers strength, stamina and physical resilience; Will covers resolve, recollection and resistance to supernatural influence. Resources represents material means. Kith & Kin represents relationships and networks that can supply support, information or leads. Record both an attribute's maximum and its damaged current rank.

Characters begin without conditions. Different conditions are cumulative; the same condition is not applied more than once. The retained core carrying limit is eight pieces of equipment.

### Fate Points

Spend a Fate Point to add +1D to a test. Spend no more than three Fate Points on one test. The core starts ordinary characters with zero Fate Points; Heroic Destiny supplies a specific exception for a successor character.

## 3. Party and character creation

<!-- Source: PLAYER24, PDF page 2. -->

Party Creation Campaigns of Grendel work best with one Gamemaster (GM) and three players, but the system can support up to six players.

Players each play one of Queen Sigrid’s Huscarls, humanity’s last bulwark against the encroaching darkness. While many Huscarls are powerful warriors and war leaders, this is not mandatory - many also specialise as skilled trackers or scouts; act as herbalists, negotiators or craftspeople; or draw upon ancient wisdom, divine omens or speak the language of runes.

Before the campaign begins, players and the GM should collaborate to determine their characters’ abilities and histories using the following process. Most sessions will begin with a ‘session zero’, where players meet to discuss their characters’ histories and complete the character creation process collaboratively.

Note that character creation involves not just determining your character’s statistics and abilities, but also their relationships and place in the game

world. Players are encouraged to create characters who have existing e relationships with one-another, and with other characters in the world. As part of creating their characters, most players will also name some number of related NPCs and situate those supporting characters in the game world.

## 7. Weapon focuses, techniques and masteries

<!-- Source: PLAYER24, PDF page 29. -->

Weapon focus As warriors grow more proficient and experienced, they tend to favour particular weapons and develop their own specialised techniques and manoeuvres.

Choosing a weapon focus Upon reaching Warrior 4 a character may nominate a weapon focus, which represents a preference or affinity towards a particular weapon. At rank 6 of Warrior, a character may choose to either add a second weapon focus, or to single-mindedly pursue their first focus and gain mastery over that weapon.

A character chooses from the following list when selecting a weapon focus:

- Axe

- Bow

- Dagger

- Shield

- Sling

- Spear

- Staff

- Sword

- Warhammer

A character may not focus on talismans, grappling hooks or nets, as these are primarily the tools of priests, thieves and sailors rather than warriors.

A weapon focus grants no benefit or advantage in itself, but it does grant access to the techniques associated with the chosen weapon, as discussed below.

Techniques and masteries Highly skilled warriors have signature moves, tactics or fighting styles that give them an edge over their opponents.

In Grendel these are referred to as techniques. Particularly effective techniques are referred to as masteries, but the same rules apply to both.

Techniques are associated with a particular weapon, and grant bonuses when certain conditions are met while using that weapon.

### Techniques only apply when using

<!-- Source: PLAYER24, PDF page 30. -->

their associated weapon to act in a fight. Unless specifically noted they have no effect at any other time.

Gaining Techniques Characters gain techniques as they develop their Warrior skill. Upon reaching certain milestones characters automatically gain their choice of techniques, as outlined below.

These benefits are cumulative. For example, a character who begins the campaign at Warrior 6 would select the focuses, masteries and techniques acquired at ranks 4, 5 and 6.

- at Warrior 4: choose a weapon focus, and one technique associated with that weapon;

- at Warrior 5: choose one mastery associated with your weapon focus;

- at Warrior 6 : choose a either second weapon focus or redouble your focus on your first weapon. Gain one technique associated with your choice;

- at Warrior 7: choose one

mastery associated with any weapon you focus on.

Any time a character could select a mastery they may instead select a technique from the same weapon.

Example: Anselm is a renowned swordsman, who begins the campaign at Warrior 6.

For Warrior 6 he can choose to either gain a second weapon focus, or to redouble his efforts with sword. Anselm wants to bolster his party’s defence, so notes shield as his second weapon focus. He must also select a technique associated with shield, so chooses Bulwark.

## 9. Mission briefing and the Adventure Phase

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 39. -->

Session Overview Sessions in Grendel follow the following structure:

- Receive the mission

- Adventuring Phase

- Community Phase

- Wrap up

Receive the mission The first order of business for the session is to establish the party’s mission. This is their objective for the session, which should be in service of the defence of the realm.

A mission may involve patrolling the borders; investigating thieves and bandits; fighting off wild beasts; repelling an attack by trolls or other outlanders; carrying a vital message through a blizzard, reconnaissance into the darkness; or countless other operations necessary to postpone the downfall of humanity.

At the start of the session the GM should prepare a short briefing scene where the party’s mission for the session is described. This may be a

formal mission received from Queen Sigrid or another figure, or simply be the natural consequence of where the party finished the last session and what they need to accomplish next.

Once an NPC has briefed the party or the mission has been established in some other way, the players should note their new objective under ‘Mission’ on their character sheet. All players will have the same mission, which might be something like:

- “Deliver the message to Sommerholm”;

- “Investigate the missing ore shipment”; or

- “Journey into the darkness and scout Yarrow Hill”.

If your session begins part way through a mission, the GM should recap the mission up to this point.

Declare your goals After the GM has assigned the mission, each player should note their character’s personal goal for

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 40. -->

the session, and for the mission more broadly.

This should be more personal than simply “complete the mission successfully”, and speak to your character’s ideals and motivations.

Although a Thegn’s goal may be “perform well on the mission to impress Queen Sigrid”, it may just as easily be “ensure the safety of the party”.

A Huscarl who favours tracking and hunting may have a goal of “lead the party safely through Steinford Forest”, while a Fledgeling’s might be “impress my mentor and learn new skills”. Once each player has noted a goal they should announce it to the table.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 41. -->

Adventuring Phase The Adventuring Phase is where the players pursue the objectives of their mission, and the GM presents roadblocks which must be overcome.

The adventuring phase can be conceptualised as a series of obstacles which stand between the party and the successful completion of their mission. Overcoming those obstacles is resolved using the method below:

1) GM presents the players with an obstacle When creating the adventure the GM will devise a number of obstacles. These are what the players will need to overcome to complete their mission, and will typically take the form of:

- overcoming beasts;

- overcoming other humans;

- overcoming monsters or outlanders;

- overcoming nature; or

- overcoming the supernatural.

An obstacle is a short, immediate barrier to the party’s objectives.

Different obstacles will test different skills and attributes, so it is important to have a well-rounded party.

The GM should devise an obstacle, and describe it to the players. For example:

“As you approach the river you notice that the bridge to Attersford has been blown away in the storm. What will you do?”

2) Players devise plan and describe attempt The party should take a moment, consult their skills and traits, and consider a plan. They should explain their plan to the GM, and suggest a skill, knowledge or attribute to use to overcome the obstacle. If there are multiple competing plans, and no consensus can be reached, then the Thegn should make the final decision.

Although Jorm the Fledgeling offers to swim across the river with a hook and line to help the party cross, the group ultimately decides that Elwyn should

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 42. -->

instead create a makeshift raft using her skills as a shipwright.

3) Test or Conflict? If a dispute between two characters is simple, quick, and not central to the narrative then it can also be resolved as an opposed test (see below). If a dispute between characters is more complex, involved or cinematic then it should be resolved as a conflict.

If an obstacle does not involve multiple parties with competing goals then it should always be resolved as a test.

As examples: an arm-wrestling contest between two characters could be resolved as a test, while a melee between two parties should be resolved as a conflict. Trying to talk your way past a guard might be resolved as a test, but the trial of an accused traitor would be resolved as a conflict.

The remaining steps below assume that the player is resolving a test. If the GM determines that a more complex dispute is occurring

between multiple parties, instead see Conflicts, page 51.

In our example, the crossing of the river is not a dispute between two different parties, so it is resolved as a test.

4) GM sets Obstacle value (Ob) Once the party has decided on a course of action, the GM determines the difficulty of the test to overcome the obstacle, and which skill, knowledge or attribute will be tested. The difficulty is represented by an obstacle value, written as ‘Ob x’, where x is the difficulty.

There are 4 methods to determine the Ob value of a test:

- improvising;

- using a seasonal value for tests involving nature or the weather;

- using an opposed test to determine a contest between multiple characters; or

- using a predetermined Ob value associated with the specific use of a skill.

### Improvising

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 43. -->

This is the most common way to set an Ob value. The GM considers the party’s circumstances, and determines the difficulty of the test using the heuristic below:

- Ob 1: commonplace

- Ob 2: challenging

- Ob 3: expert

- Ob 4: master

- Ob 5: heroic

A significant factor which complicates the test or assists the party might add or subtract from this Ob value.

As Elwyn has all the required materials to make a raft, but is out in the field rather than in a workshop, the GM decides that this will be a challenging task. Normally for a task of this difficulty the GM would assign a Craftsperson (Shipwright) test with an Ob value of 2. However the tide is high and the river is moving quickly, which means the raft will need to be more stable than usual. The GM increases the Ob value by one due to these complicating factors, resulting in

an Ob 3 Craftsperson (shipwright) test.

Consult the season Each season has an Ob value associated with it. If your test is to overcome the elements or a feature of the natural world then the GM should use the value below:

- Spring: 4

- Summer: 3

- Autumn: 3

- Winter: 6 For example, this value might be used if a character tries to navigate through a blizzard or find shelter in a storm.

If the party had decided to follow Jorm’s plan of swimming across the fast-flowing spring river, the GM might have assigned an Ob 4 Vigour test.

Opposed test If the test is attempting to overcome another character, and the dispute between the two characters is simple and quick to resolve, then there is no need to decide an Ob value. Instead,

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 44. -->

both characters make an appropriate test and compare their results. This is discussed in further detail below (see Opposed Tests, page 46).

Specific skills Certain skills have specific Ob values associated with certain uses in the Community Phase (see page 79). If so, simply use the Ob value listed for that skill.

5) Gather dice pool Resolving tests in Grendel requires players or the GM to roll multiple six-sided dice (D6) at once. This is called your ‘dice pool’, and the number of dice you roll for any given test is based on a variety of factors, as discussed below:

- Your expertise or natural ability;

- Help from skills and knowledges;

- Help from teammates;

- Help from equipment;

- Help or hindrance from traits.

Adding a die to your dice pool is presented as ‘+1D’, while subtracting a die is presented as ‘-1D’.

Your expertise The GM will tell you which skill, attribute or Knowledge you need to test. Find your rating for that skill, attribute or Knowledge, and add that many dice to your die pool.

In our example, the GM has asked Elwyn to make a Craftsperson (shipwright) check to create the raft. Elwyn has a Craftsperson (shipwright) rating of 3, so adds +3D to her dice pool.

Help from skills and knowledges If you are making a test, and your character has another skill or knowledge which the group decides is appropriate to the task at hand (and which is not the skill or knowledge already being tested), then you may add +1D to your dice pool.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 45. -->

You may only claim this bonus die once, regardless of the number of relevant skills and knowledges you have.

Elwyn also has the Ships and the Sea knowledge which could be called on to add a further +1D, taking her dice pool to four.

Help from equipment If your character has a tool from the equipment list which the group decides is appropriate to the task at hand, you may add +1D to your test.

Only one piece of equipment may be used per test, and you must describe how the equipment is helping you. Elwyn carries shipwright’s tools with her, adding a further +1D, taking her total to five dice.

Help from teamwork Other members of the party can offer to help with a test, using the following process:

- If you are making a skill test, any character with a relevant

skill or knowledge may offer help;

- If you are making a knowledge test, any player may help with a relevant knowledge may offer help;

- If you are making an attribute test, any player can help.

Add +1D to your test for each other player that helps. Each other player can add a maximum of +1D. In each case they must describe how their skill, knowledge or attribute assists you to make your test.

Any character who provides a teamwork die also binds themselves to the outcome of your test, pass or fail. If your failed test results in you suffering a condition (such as injured or exhausted) or other penalty, all helping characters also suffer this consequence.

No other players have any skills relating to building a raft, so Elwyn’s dice pool remains at five. If another party member had a related skill or knowledge like Craftsperson

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 46. -->

(shipwright or carpenter) or Ships and the Sea they may have been able to help.

Traits Players may voluntarily use traits to either help or hinder their character, adding or subtracting dice from the dice pool. See Using Traits on page 49 for further details. 6) Determine pass or fail Once you have gathered your dice pool, roll the dice! Any die with a result of 1-3 counts as a failure, while any result of 4+ counts as a success.

In addition to counting as a success, any die which rolls a 6 can also be rerolled, adding a further success if the reroll is a 4+. You can continue rerolling the same die as long as you continue rolling sixes.

Some pieces of equipment grant ‘+1S’ to a test. This adds one to your total number of successes, after all dice have been rolled.

Elwyn rolls her five dice, with the results of 1, 1, 3, 5, 6. Two successes and three failures, with one reroll. Elwyn’s

rerolled die rolls a 4, leaving her with a total of three successes.

Independent tests If the test was not directly competing with another character, it is considered an ‘independent’ test. If your number of successes is equal to or greater than the Ob value set by the GM then you pass the test. You should narrate how you overcame the challenge, as appropriate to your margin of success.

Elwyn rolled three successes against an Ob 3 test, so she succeeded in building a raft stable enough to cross the river. As she had a margin of success of zero, her player describes the raft as shaky and rickety, but still safe enough to make the crossing.

Opposed tests If the test was against another character, it is considered an ‘opposed’ test. Both characters roll their dice pools using the method described above. Whoever rolls the most successes wins the test — the

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 47. -->

greater the difference between the number of successes, the greater the margin of victory.

The victor of the test should narrate what happened during the test and how they succeeded, as appropriate to the margin of success.

In the event of a tie, compare the ratings of the skills, knowledges or attributes the characters were testing, with the higher rating acting as tiebreaker. If both characters have the same rating, then the next tiebreaker is whoever rolled the most dice in their dice pool. If this is also tied then the characters are truly at a deadlock.

Success If you passed the test then you should narrate what your character did to achieve their goal, consistent with the margin of success.

Failure If you fail the test, the GM describes what happens to your character.

Generally, in narrative terms, failure of a test doesn’t necessarily mean that the character failed to reach their goal. It can simply mean that there were unexpected consequences, new challenges which emerged during the attempt, or that it took a toll on the character who made the attempt.

The GM has a choice of three possible consequences to apply when a player fails a test: create a twist, impose a condition, or lower an attribute.

Twists A twist is an unexpected change of circumstances, which occurs when the character makes their attempt. This is an opportunity for the GM to introduce new gameplay or narrative elements. Generally a twist will either be:

- a further challenge (and further tests) for the characters to overcome before the mission can progress;

- a conflict which breaks out; or

- A change in the weather (see Weather Twists, page 83).

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 48. -->

If Elwyn had failed her test, the GM might narrate that the raft made it across the river, but add the twist that Elwyn fell overboard during the voyage, and must make an Ob 4 Vigour test to swim ashore.

Generally if the test for a twist is also failed, the GM should impose a condition or have a conflict erupt, rather than add further twists.

Impose a condition If you fail a test the GM, rather than creating a twist, may instead allow you to succeed at your goal but also impose a condition on your character. The condition imposed should generally be most appropriate to the task at hand: Exhausted, Injured, Unnerved, Famished, or Sick.

If Elwyn had fallen overboard during her voyage, and then failed the Vigour test to swim ashore, the GM might narrate that she wore herself out while swimming and now suffers from the Exhausted condition.

Conditions impose ongoing penalties on a character. Details on how to recover from conditions can be found on page 77.

A description of each condition is below:

Famished Your character has not had access to adequate food and/or water. Your character suffers -1 Disposition in any conflict (see Conflicts, page 51).

Exhausted Your character has physically overexerted themselves. Your character suffers -1 to Disposition in any conflict.

Injured Your character has suffered a wound or other injury. Your character suffers -1D to any skill test, as well as any Vigour, Will or Cunning test. This penalty does not apply to tests for recovery.

### Sick

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 49. -->

Your character has contracted an illness. Your character suffers -1D to any skill test, as well as any Vigour, Will or Cunning test. This penalty does not apply to tests for recovery.

Unnerved Your character has had their morale broken, or has been terrified by supernatural horrors. Your character suffers -1D to Disposition to any conflict.

Dead Your character has departed the mortal world to rejoin their ancestors. Your character is retired (see page 86). The Dead condition may not be applied unless a character suffers from all five other conditions, as a result of a conflict where one side kills the other, or due to extremely high-risk weather.

Lower an attribute If the test which a character failed was an attribute test (Vigour, Will, Cunning, Resources, Kith & Kin), then the GM may temporarily lower the attribute tested by one point,

rather than imposing a condition. The player should make a note of both the new and original scores on their character sheet.

Lowered attributes can be refreshed to their original value when certain criteria are met, as discussed on page 81.

As examples, a character who fails a Resources test may have spent more than expected, so is short of coin until they return home. A character who failed a Vigour test may have strained a tendon, and be less capable until they’ve rested.

## 10. Using traits

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 50. -->

Using Traits Characters in Grendel have a number of traits, which describe their personal qualities, attributes and attitudes.

A character may call on a trait at any time during the Adventure Phase of a mission, to either help with a test they are about to take, or to hinder themselves in a test they’re about to take.

If a player wishes to use a trait to hinder or help during a test, they should explain to the group how the trait is applicable. If the group agrees that the trait is relevant to the task at hand then the player may use the trait.

Only one trait may be used to influence any test, and only for one effect.

The Huscarls need to make a Will test to resist the nightmarish whisper of a mare. The table agrees that Bjaern’s Stoic trait is appropriate to help him maintain his composure.

Jorm needs to broker a compromise with a local noble, and puts it to the table that his Stubborn trait would hinder his efforts. The table likewise agrees that being stubborn would negatively impact negotiations, so Jorm may apply his trait.

Using a Trait to help You can use a trait to help in three different ways, depending on your rating in that Trait:

Rating 1 If you have rating 1 in a trait, then once per Adventure Phase you may call upon it to add +1D to a relevant test you are about to make.

If you use this, mark the appropriate circle on your character sheet as a reminder.

Rating 2 If you have rating 2 in a trait, then you may add +1D to every relevant test you make in the Adventure Phase.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 51. -->

Rating 3 If you have rating 3 in a trait, you gain the rating 2 benefit as well the benefit below:

Once per Adventure Phase you may reroll all dice that you rolled when making an appropriate test.

If you use this, mark the appropriate circle on your character sheet as a reminder.

Using a Trait to hinder You may also use a trait to hinder yourself. In addition to the roleplaying reasons, every time a player draws on a trait to hurt their own prospects they gain a number of bonus actions in the Community Phase (see page 76).

You may hinder yourself in three ways (any of the three may be used, regardless of your rating in a trait, but only one per test).

In every case you should mark the actions you earn in the Traits section of your character sheet.

Hurt own chances You may use a trait to give yourself -1D on an appropriate test. If so, gain 1 action in the next Community Phase.

Help an opponent In an opposed test, you may call upon an applicable trait to give your opponent +2D. This grants 2 actions in the next Community Phase.

Break tie In an opposed test, if you and your opponent roll the same number of successes you may invoke an appropriate trait to break the tie in their favour with a margin of success of 1. If so you earn 2 actions for the next Community Phase.

## 11. Conflicts

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 52. -->

Conflicts For complex disputes between one or more characters (or groups of characters) the conflict rules should be used. A conflict can represent anything from combat to a chase scene to an intense argument; and each can be resolved using the same fundamental mechanics.

Specific rules for the four most common types of conflict (fights, chases, debates, battles) can be found later in this section (see page 66).

Multiple characters on the same side of a conflict form a team and act as a cohesive whole. In a fight, multiple party members act more like a unit than as individual actors. Grendel is a cooperative game, and the party either lives or dies together.

The same general process for resolving conflicts is followed in all instances, using the following steps:

1) Describe conflict, note participants, teams and goals The first step is to determine which character is on each side of the conflict and what they want to achieve. Divide the participants into teams with shared goals, and note the teams on the conflict sheet

Afterwards, each team declares their goal: what they’re trying to achieve by the end of the conflict.

The party can deliberate to decide which characters are participating in the conflict, and to make sure their goals align.

Example: the party have tracked the bandits to their forest camp, but they’ve just been spotted and a skirmish is about to break out. The type of conflict is noted as a fight.

Participants on the party’s team are noted as “the Huscarls Elwyn, Jorm and Bjaern”, while the NPC team is noted as “the Elpin Glade bandits”.

The player’s deliberate, and decide that they’d like to bring the bandits in alive

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 53. -->

for questioning. As such the party’s goal is noted on the conflict sheet as “subdue the bandits”, while the bandits’ goal is noted as “kill the Huscarls”.

2) Determine starting Disposition Disposition is a measure of each team’s determination, resolve, and capacity to continue the conflict.

As examples: in a battle, Disposition might measure the participants’ endurance and morale, while in an argument Disposition might be a measure of each party’s tenacity and the conviction of their beliefs.

Disposition can also be viewed as a measure of how close each side in the conflict is to achieving their goal. Throughout the conflict the Disposition value of each side will rise and fall as a result of their actions and those of their opponents. When the Disposition of one side reaches zero, the other side has accomplished their goal!

Starting Disposition is determined by each team nominating a leader.

Depending on the type of conflict, the leader makes a skill test and adds the number of successes to their rating in a particular base attribute, as shown in the table below. This be the team’s starting Disposition. As with other tests, additional dice may be added for equipment, knowledges, Traits and teamwork.

Each other character who is participating on a team must add a teamwork die to the leader’s Disposition test - if you’re not helping then you’re not part of the team. Team members may always add this teamwork die regardless of the skill being rolled.

The party nominates Bjaern, their most formidable warrior, as their leader for the fight. As such, Bjaern is the character who determines the party’s starting Disposition.

Bjaern has a Warrior rating of 6, and each of the other two party members adds one additional die for teamwork. Bjaern rolls his pool of eight dice, and rolls five successes. He adds these to his Vigour rating of 5, and also adds an

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 54. -->

additional success due to his mail armour. The party team’s starting Disposition is 11.

The four bandits all have identical skills and attributes. They have a Warrior rating of 4, three extra dice from teamwork, and are wearing leather armour which adds a further +1D. They roll their dice pool of eight, and roll five successes. These are added to their base Vigour score (4), for a total starting Disposition of nine.

With a Disposition of eleven compared to the bandits’ nine, the party are slightly better positioned for the action ahead.

3) Conflict Rounds Conflicts are made up of a number of rounds, each of which contains three actions (opportunities for members of each opposing side to act).

The aim of a conflict is for each side to deplete their opponents’ Disposition. Rounds continue until one side reaches zero Disposition, which signals the end of the conflict. When this happens, the team which successfully depleted their opponents’ Disposition reaches their goal.

(For the below description we will assume that the player’s team will include either three or four characters. For teams with more or fewer than this number see Advanced Conflict Rules on page 72).

4) Preparing actions Each round of the conflict consists of three actions, which are scripted secretly by either side of the conflict at the start of each round, then revealed and resolved in order.

For actions 1, 2, and 3 of the round, the GM should secretly note which NPC will act, what they will do during that action, and which weapon or equipment they will use.

After the GM has secretly noted the NPC actions, the party should discuss which member will act during each action (actions 1, 2 and 3), what they do with their action, and which weapon or equipment they will use. Each party member may only act once per conflict round.

(Note that if this is the second or subsequent round of a conflict, the character who performs action 1 for a team may not be the same character who performed action 3 in the previous round.)

Once both sides have made a note of their actions and equipment, the actions for the round are revealed simultaneously and resolved in order.

The Huscarls have been spotted! GM secretly decides that the enemy actions will be 1) Bandit A will attack with his

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 56. -->

bow, 2) Bandit B will Defend with his sword, and 3) Bandit C will attack with his Sword.

The party decides that in order their actions will be 1) Elwyn will Manoeuvre with her bow, 2) Bjaern will Attack with his axe and 3) Jorm will Attack with his sword.

These scripted actions are revealed simultaneously, and resolved in order ( also simultaneously). Firstly Elwyn and Bandit A resolve their actions, then Jorm and Bandit B, then finally Bjaern and Bandit C. If the conflict has not concluded at the end of these three actions then a new round will begin.

Note: Teams with four characters If a team has four characters, then one character will have to sit out each round of the conflict (ie, not get a chance to act). The same character may not sit out multiple turns in a row. Characters who are sitting out may still add teamwork dice.

5) Actions In a conflict, there is a choice of four actions you can take: Attack, Defend,

Manoeuvre and Feint. These four actions are consistent across every type of conflict (whether a fight, chase, battle or a debate), but what each action represents varies depending on the specific context, as discussed below.

Each action represents more than a moment in time (more than a single thrust of a sword or sentence uttered, for example), and rather represents a short scene or sequence of events.

Different actions require particular skill tests as appropriate to the conflict. You can see which skills are required for each action in the table on page 61.

Each action and its uses is described below:

Attack An Attack is an attempt to achieve your team’s goal (as noted on the conflict sheet) directly. A successful Attack will lower the enemy team’s Disposition.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 57. -->

In a fight, an Attack action might be hacking at the enemy with your weapon. In a chase where the party is pursuing its enemies, an Attack might represent sprinting directly towards your prey. An Attack in an argument might represent stridently setting out your case.

To Attack, make a skill test as appropriate for the conflict, (see page 61). For example, in a fight you attack by making a Warrior test. An Attack is typically an independent Ob 0 test, but may be resolved as an opposed test depending on your opponent’s action (see below). Each margin of success lowers the other team’s Disposition by one point.

Defend A Defend action is an attempt to prevent the enemy from achieving their goals (as noted on the conflict sheet). A successful Defend action will either prevent the enemy from lowering your Disposition (if they’re trying to Attack), or recover lost Disposition.

In a fight, a Defend action might represent literally blocking or deflecting attacks, but it might equally represent ducking behind cover to quickly gather your wits or attend to a wound. In an argument, a Defend action might represent restating a strong argument, or rebutting the point your opponent just made.

To Defend, make an Ob 3 test as appropriate for the conflict (as an example, in a fight you Defend by making an Cunning test). Each margin of success raises your team’s Disposition by one point, but not above your starting Disposition.

If you Defend while your opponent is Attacking or Manoeuvring, make an opposed test instead. If you win then you recover Disposition equal to your margin of success.

Feint The Feint action is an attempt to open the enemy up for a devastating blow. A Feint is a high-risk, high-reward action, that aims to quickly end the conflict in your

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 58. -->

favor. A feint may be disrupted or intercepted by your opponent though, leading to catastrophic consequences.

In a fight, a Feint might be a literal feint to slip past your opponent’s guard. In a battle, a Feint might be a flanking manoeuvre or ambush. In an argument, a Feint might be luring your opponent into an admission of guilt or trapping them in an inconsistency.

If you Feint when your Opponent Attacks, the Feint is intercepted by the enemy attack and is unsuccessful! Your opponent resolves their Attack as normal, and you skip your action.

If you Feint when your opponent Defends, then you have intercepted your opponent and the Feint is successful. Your opponent skips their action, and you roll an Ob 0 skill test as appropriate to the conflict (as an example, in a fight you Feint by making a Warrior test). Each succes reduces the enemy Disposition by one point.

If you Feint while your opponent Manoeuvres, make an independent test. Reduce the enemy Disposition by your margin of success.

Manoeuvre A Manoeuvre action is an attempt to improve your team’s position, to impede or disrupt your enemy, or to remove one of your enemy’s advantages. Manoeuvre does not directly change either team’s Disposition, but does either provide your team with bonuses to their next test or impose penalties on your opponents.

In a fight, a Manoeuvre might be an attempt to trip or disarm your opponent, or to lay down suppressing fire with your bow. In a chase, a Manoeuvre might be knocking over an obstacle to impede your pursuer. In a battle, a Manoeuvre might be a literal manoeuvre to high ground, or cutting off the enemy supply lines. In a debate, a Manoeuvre might be laying the groundwork for a point you’ll return to later, or pre-empting your opponent’s argument.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 59. -->

When you take a Manoeuvre action, if your opponent Attacks or Defends make an opposed test. If your opponent Feints or Manoeuvres make an Ob 0 independent test using the appropriate skill (as an example, in a fight you Manoeuvre using Cunning).

Your margin of success determines the type and number of effects you can impose on the conflict. Each success can be ‘spent’ to achieve a combination of the below effects (each effect may only be chosen once per action):

Impede - 1 success You create an obstacle for your opponents. The opposing team suffers -1D to their next test.

Gain position - 2 successes You provide some positive benefit to your team. Add +2D to the next test your team takes.

Disarm - 3 successes You remove or neutralise one of the enemy team’s advantages.

Choose one piece of enemy equipment: it may not be used for the remainder of the conflict.

6) End of round After three actions have been resolved the conflict round has ended. If no side of the conflict has had their Disposition reduced to zero then a new round begins.

If one side has their Disposition reduced to zero then the conflict immediately ends, see Costs of Victory, below.

Tests in conflicts Tests in conflicts are resolved the same way as tests in any other setting. The dice pool is assembled and rolled as normal, except that:

- you can only claim a bonus from equipment which is specific to that type of conflict (weapons for a fight, military advantages for a battle, etc, detailed below); and

- the only characters who can offer a teamwork die for your

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 60. -->

dice pool are characters who are part of your team in the conflict.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 61. -->

Example conflict round During Action 1 of the round, Bandit A Attacks with his bow and Elwyn Manoeuvres with her bow. This is an opposed test (Elwyn’s Cunning vs the bandit’s Warrior) so both sides roll their dice pools and compare: 4 successes vs 2 in Elwyn’s favour. Elwyn spends her Manoeuvre successes to ‘gain position’, adding +2D to the team’s next test. Elwyn’s player narrates this as Elwyn being quicker on the draw, and keeping the bandits pinned down with bowfire while the rest of the party charge.

Action 2 of the round sees Bandit B Defending with his sword and Bjaern Attacking with his axe. This is another opposed roll, Bjaern’s Warrior vs the bandit’s Cunning. Thanks to the advantage given by Elwyn, Bjaern is able to easily triumph: 5 successes vs 2. Bjaern’s axe adds a further success due to its Deadly quality, taking the Huscarls’ total margin of success to 4.

The bandits lose four points of Disposition, lowering their total from 9 to 5. Bjaern’s player narrates this as the Huscarl falling upon the bandit’s

position, and smashing two of them to the ground with the flat of his axe.

Action 3 sees Jorm Attacking with his sword while Bandit C also Attacks with his sword. These are independent tests, so both characters get a chance to act. Jorm rolls 3 successes on his Warrior test, further lowering the bandits’ Disposition from 5 to 2. Jorm’s player narrates this as the Fledgeling pouncing on one of the prone bandits, placing a blade to his throat and calling for his surrender.

Meanwhile Bandit C rolls well for his Warrior test, and scores four successes in return. This Attack lowers the Huscarls’ Disposition from 11 to 7. The GM narrates it as the prone bandit slashing out wildly and slicing Jorm’s hamstring as the Fledgeling makes his move. At the end of conflict round 1 the Huscarls are at 7 Disposition, while the bandits are at 2. Things are looking grim for the bandits of Elpin Glade! It’s likely that the party will achieve their goal: to subdue the bandits for questioning.

Cost of victory When one side’s Disposition reaches zero, their opponents have won the conflict and accomplished their goal!

Before the mission can continue, however, certain costs of victory must be conceded, depending on how close the losing team got to achieving their goal.

If the winning team’s Disposition at the end of the conflict is equal to its starting value, then victory was unconditional. The mission resumes

### = see Feint description

as normal with the winners achieving their goal.

If the winning team is missing Disposition, however, then they must make some concessions to the losers. The most common way is by imposing conditions on the winner, depending on the margin of victory:

Concession If the winning team lost only a few points of Disposition, then the losers may call for a concession. The losers may inflict the Exhausted or Famished condition on each member of the winning team.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 64. -->

Compromise If the winning team lost about half of its starting Disposition, then the losers may call for a compromise. The losers may inflict the Injured, Unnerved, or Sick condition on each member of the winning team.

Major compromise If the winners lost all but a few of their starting Disposition, the losers may call for a major compromise.

The losers may inflict both:

- Exhausted or Famished; and

- Injured, Sick or Unnerved on each member of the winning team.

Resolving cost of victory The losing team proposes the conditions to the winning team, and then the table comes to a consensus as to whether or not it’s fair.

Note that the three categories are a continuum, not thresholds, and

should bleed into one another where appropriate. For example, a winning team which lost over half its starting Disposition might accrue conditions appropriate for a compromise on all team members except for one or two, who instead accrue conditions appropriate for a major compromise. These characters narratively bore the brunt of it during the conflict, and pay the price afterwards.

After reaching a consensus on the conditions, the mission resumes with the winning side narrating their victory.

Debates and conditions Note that the infliction of conditions like Exhausted or Injured is more appropriate to physical conflicts like fights and chases. For interpersonal conflicts, more narrative or material concessions might be made as the cost of victory. .

For example, a concession in a trade negotiation might mean that the final agreed price is slightly lower than the winner expected. A major

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 65. -->

concession in a criminal trial might mean that the guilty party is imprisoned rather than put to death.

These types of conditions should be consistent with the conflict at hand. When deciding an appropriate balance of conditions, terms like ‘compromise’ and ‘concession’ should be read as their plain English meanings.

Conflict outcomes example Round 2 of the conflict starts with the Huscarls at Disposition 7, and the bandits at Disposition 2.

The bandits know that they need to quickly improve their Disposition so that they don’t get captured, and the only way to do that is through the Defend action. As such, the GM secretly notes a defensive series of actions: 1) Bandit B Defends with his sword, 2) Bandit C Defends with his sword, 3) Bandit A Manoeuvres with his bow.

The players predict this, however, and plan to decisively end the conflict: 1) Elwyn Feints with her bow; 2) Bjaern

Attacks with his axe, 3) Jorm attacks with his sword. The first action is revealed: Elwyn’s Feint against Bandit’s B’s Defend. The bandit falls for the Feint and is unable to act! Elwyn makes an independent Warrior test and scores 3 successes, enough to lower the bandits’ Disposition to 0. Elwyn’s player narrates this as her using the distraction of the melee to creep through the underbrush, emerge with her bow knocked behind the bandits, and demand their surrender.

As one side’s Disposition has been lowered to zero, the conflict immediately ends - the remaining actions this turn are not resolved.

The party has achieved its goal: the bandits are subdued, and can now be questioned. Before that is dealt with though, costs of victory must be determined.

The Huscarls lost 4 of their starting 11 Disposition, which is at the high end of a Concession, but not quite enough to warrant a Compromise.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 66. -->

The GM proposes that Elwyn and Bjaern accrue the Exhausted condition, and that Jorm accrues the Injured condition. The GM presents these conditions as being consistent with the level of Disposition lost, as well as the narrated actions of the fight.

The players take a moment to consider and discuss within the group, before agreeing and noting the conditions on their character sheets.

Killing If your conflict is a fight or a battle, and your goal is to kill your opponent or destroy the enemy army, then certain conditions must be met throughout the conflict to achieve your goal.

The following conditions apply in addition to the other costs of victory above:

- In order to cleanly kill your opponent(s), you must win with no Disposition lost;

- If you win with a concession, then your opponent gets one

last action before they die: a soliloquy, a final act of defiance, staggering off into the woods to die in dignity, etc;

- If you win with a compromise, in a group or battle setting, then roughly half of your opponents are dead, but the rest flee and accrue the Injured and Exhausted conditions;

- If you win with a compromise in a one-on-one duel, then the opponent can beg for mercy. How you resolve this may have reputational or narrative effects;

- If you win with a major compromise, then your opponents are left for dead but can regroup. The survivors accrue every condition, and reduce their Resources and Kith & Kin by 1. In a group setting roughly one quarter of the opponents survive.

<a id="12-fights-battles-chases-and-debates"></a>

## 12. Fights, battles, chases and debates

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 67. -->

Specific conflicts There are four major types of conflicts: fights, chases, debates, and battles. Each has unique features and specific equipment which will be discussed below.

Fights Fights represent combats or other physical confrontations between up to ten participants on either side. Conflicts larger than that should be resolved as a battle instead.

Range Fights are spatial conflicts, and having a longer range than your opponent can give you an advantage. There are three range bands:

- Melee

- Reach

- Missile

If you Attack an opponent whose weapon has a different range to yours, you make an opposed test if they Attack and an independent test if they Manoeuvre.

Weapons and Armour Weapons and armour are appropriate equipment in a fight. See the Equipment section (see page 35) for details.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 68. -->

Battles Larger military engagements are resolved as battles. These conflicts might be between forces as small as a dozen warriors or armies as large as a few thousand. A battle might be a siege, a pitched battle, a raid, or really an engagement of any scale.

Player characters who join a team during a battle are considered to take part in the command section: issuing orders, planning logistics, or offering runic support. Characters who do not have skills in these areas may wish to sit out the conflict, or alternatively may join the ranks of a host and fight directly as heroes (see below).

Hosts In addition to the other considerations below, the number of troops each side is commanding is one of the primary factors when determining the outcome of a battle.The forces on either side are referred to as hosts.

Hosts are broken up into four rough size bands, representing different

magnitudes of forces and engagements.

Each size of host has a Weight-Of-Numbers (WON) rating attached which is described in detail below:

- Retinue: up to a dozen warriors, WON 0

- Warband: up to fifty warriors, WON 2

- Battalion: up to several hundred warriors, WON 5

- Army: up to several thousand warriors, WON 9

Weight of numbers Each host has a WON rating attached to it, which represents the number of troops available to fight. This has a number of uses during the conflict:

1) Add your weight of numbers rating to your starting Disposition; 2) If two forces of different sizes are in opposition, subtract the smaller WON rating from the larger. This is the size differential between the

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 69. -->

forces. The larger force adds +xD to every test in the conflict, where x is the size differential.

Sometimes a military force consists of multiple hosts working together (for example, a warband of militia accompanied by a retinue of Huscarls). If so, the combined force’s WON is equal to the WON value of the largest force +1 (the maximum modifier is +1, regardless of the number of allied forces).

Military advantages The following military advantages can be used as equipment during a battle. Unlike other conflicts, more than one military advantage may be claimed for any given test.

Take note at the start of a battle which advantages each army has:

Heroes Characters who are more practically-inclined may join the host as a hero. The hero may not be assigned as a team member in the

conflict (ie, may not be part of the host’s command section). Whenever the host Attacks, one attached hero may make an Ob 3 Warrior test (or Hunter test if the Attack is against a monster). The host gains +1D to its Attack test for each success.

Whenever the host Manoeuvres, one attached hero may make an Ob 3 Infiltrator or Pathfinder test. The host gains +1D to its Manoeuvre test for each success.

Heroes bind themselves to the consequences of the conflict, but may not offer teamwork dice.

Command structure If your host is a battalion or army, you can claim a command structure if you have designated ranks and responsibilities, and a way to communicate and coordinate orders.

A command structure grants +1D to Attack.

### Elite troops

## 13. Advanced conflict rules

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 74. -->

Advanced conflict rules It’s only necessary to read this section ahead of time if you plan to be a GM or teach the game to a new group. It will be significantly easier to understand this section after you have played a few conflicts.

Multiple teams At the start of the conflict, the party arranges themselves into teams. For a party of three players or fewer, the entire party will always be on the same team, however for larger parties they may be split into two teams. NPCs may also group into one, two or more teams, typically of no more than three to four characters.

Each team of characters works as its own unit, determines its own Disposition, and acts independently of other teams on its side.

- Parties of three or fewer are always on the same team;

- Parties of four might be in two teams of two, or one team of four;

- Parties of five are always in one team of two and one team of three;

- Parties of six are always in two teams of three.

Multiple teams on the same side If multiple teams in a conflict have the same broad agenda, they are considered to be on the same side and should have the same goal for the conflict.

Teams each script their own actions independently of one other, and reveal and resolve their actions simultaneously. The discussion below is using the assumption of two player teams and one NPC team (as this is the most likely situation), but the same principles apply to any number of teams per side. .

Once actions are revealed, if one or both player teams makes an independent test against the NPC team, then all teams act normally and all actions are resolved using the standard rules.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 75. -->

(If facing off against multiple opposing teams, independent tests affect only one target team. )

However, if both player teams would make an opposed test against the NPC team, then the player teams coordinate their actions together. The party should select one player team to be the lead team, and the other player team to be the support team. Only the lead team resolves their action, however they get +1D from teamwork for each member of the support team.

Teams with fewer characters If a team participating in a conflict has only one character, then that character simply acts for all three actions during the round.

If a team has two characters, Character A and Character B, then they will always alternate actions. In round one, the sequence of actions will be A-B-A, then in round two it will be B-A-B, and so on until the end of the conflict.

If a character acts more than once during a round they must use the same weapon for each action that round.

## 15. Character growth and learning

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 76. -->

### Character Growth

Skill and attribute development Each time you test a skill, knowledge or attribute you should note the result next to it on your character sheet, by shading the ‘P’ circle in pencil if you passed the test and the ‘F’ circle if you failed it.

Once you have passed tests equal to your current rating, and have also failed tests equal to your current rating minus 1, your rating in the skill immediately improves. Erase all shaded circles and increase your rating by 1.

(For example, a character with Sailor 3 must pass three Sailor tests and fail two Sailor tests to advance their ranking in the skill.)

Note that conflicts may require characters to test the same skill multiple times. Make a note of the result of a test only the first time a skill is tested in any conflict. Do not make a note of subsequent tests of the same skill.

Fledgelings are only required to have passed a number of times equal to their current rating minus 1. For example, a Fledgeling with Pathfinder 3 would advance after passing two Pathfinder tests and failing two tests.

Beginner’s luck and acquiring new skills If your character is required to make a test for a skill they do not know, they instead test the following:

- Cunning for skills involving subtlety, guile, perception or agility;

- Vigour for skills involving athleticism, resilience, labour, fighting, or strength; and

- Will for skills involving reason, learning, religion, relationships, or the supernatural.

Total up the dice pool for the above attribute (including equipment, teamwork and any other bonuses as normal), and make the roll using half of that number of dice.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 77. -->

After the test has been resolved, pass or fail, note the skill you attempted to use on your character sheet (with a skill rating of zero), and fill in one of the pass circles (it doesn’t matter if you passed or failed, you simply need to keep track of how many times you use the skill untrained). Each subsequent time you use the skill untrained fill in an additional pass circle, whether the test is passed or failed

When you’ve used an untrained skill a number of times equal to 10 minus your current Cunning (for example, six times for an Cunning 4 character) you learn the skill. Erase all the pass circles, and note a new rating of 2 next to the skill.

Fledgelings require only 8 uses minus their current Cunning to learn a skill (so three times for an Cunning 5

Fledgeling).

## 16. Community Phase framework and recovery

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 78. -->

Community Phase The Adventuring Phase ends once the players have accomplished their mission, achieved a milestone, or returned to a safe haven after a significant time in the field. In rare instances of protracted operations the Community Phase may take place while the party is still in the field.

After the Adventuring Phase concludes, the Community Phase begins. This is the opportunity for the characters to recover from injury, hone their skills, and prepare for the trials of their next mission.

Each player begins the Community Phase with one action. They may use this action as they see fit, choosing from the list below.

Players also gain one additional Community Phase action for each time during the preceding Adventuring Phase they voluntarily suffered a penalty as a result of a trait (see Using Traits, page 49).

Erase the actions circles in the Traits section of your character sheet at the

start of each Community Phase, and gain one action this phase per circle erased. Actions may be spent to:

- Recover from one condition;

- Purchase equipment;

- Help another player;

- Take any one skill, knowledge or attribute test; or

- Use a specific skill’s Community Phase Activity (see below).

Some skills have specific Activities they can perform during Community Phases. These are:

### Skill Activity

### Craftsperson: Crafting

### Healer: Treat ailment

### Instructor: Training

### Orator: Inspiration

### Seer: Omens

### Seer: Portents

Each of the above Activities may be performed only once per Community Phase. Other actions can be performed multiple times.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 79. -->

No player may take an action twice in a row during the Community Phase - the party is encouraged to share screentime!

Recover from conditions Take an action to attempt to recover from one condition which afflicts you. Different conditions are recovered using different processes, as described below.

There is a specific order from which conditions must be recovered (from top to bottom on your character sheet):

Famished > Exhausted > Injured > Sick > Unnerved

Note that other players may not use teamwork dice to assist with your recovery, however the Healer skill may be used to help the party recover, as described later in this section.

### Famished

You must find a nutritious meal. If you are in civilisation this requires an Ob 2 Cook, Baker, Resources or Kith & Kin test. If you are in the field and have access to a cookpot and utensils this requires an Ob 3 Survivalist test followed by an Ob 2 Cook test. Another player may spend an action to take either test in your place.

Exhausted You must find somewhere you can rest uninterrupted. If you are in civilisation this requires an Ob 2 Resources or Kith & Kin test. If you are in the field this requires an Ob 3 Vigour test.

Injured You must attempt to heal from your wounds, with or without treatment. This requires an Ob 3 Vigour test.

If the test is failed, your character suffers long-term complications from the injury. On a failure the injured status is removed, then the GM permanently lowers the rating of your Vigour, Will or Cunning by one, as appropriate to the type of injury.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 80. -->

This attribute point may not be recovered, but the attribute can be increased again using the normal process for rating improvement (see Character Growth, page 74).

Sick You must attempt to shake off your illness, with or without treatment. This requires an Ob 3 Vigour test.

If the test is failed, your character suffers long-term complications from the illness. On failure the injured status is removed, then the GM permanently lowers the rating of your Vigour, Will or Cunning by one, as appropriate to the nature of the illness. This attribute point may not be recovered, but the attribute may be increased again using the normal process for rating improvement.

Unnerved You must collect your resolve. This may involve prayer, seeking counsel from your mentor, hard labour to take out your frustrations, or anything else. The player describes what they do to recover.

Recovering from the Unnerved condition requires an Ob 2 Will test. Skill, Knowledge or Attribute check You may spend an action to make any other skill, knowledge or attribute test of your choice, to achieve any purpose.

If you have no particular reason to make a test, but instead want to practice (and potentially shade a pass/fail circle on your character sheet), you can simply make a test with no purpose and suffer no consequences for the results.

Purchase equipment You may outfit the party with weapons, army or other equipment by making a Resource test as described below. Each test may purchase one type of equipment:

Weapons and/or armour

- outfit individual: Ob 2

- outfit party: Ob 4

Other equipment (not animals)

- Outfit individual: Ob 1

- Outfit party: Ob 3

## 19. Runic lore and the Burden of the Runes

<!-- Source: PLAYER24, PDF page 48. -->

Runic Lore All characters with the Runespeaker skill can use it to:

- fight and battle versus outlanders;

- seal corrupted sites and relics; and

- interact with runic artifacts or rituals.

Additionally, each character with ranks in Runespeaker gains access to a small selection of other supernatural or religious effects, called runic lore.

Runic lore represents ancient knowledge connected to the seasons and the gods, and expressed through the almost-forgotten runic alphabet. Runic lore may be invoked through the course of play using the Runespeaker skill to achieve a variety of effects, detailed below.

### Learning runic lore

When you gain the Runespeaker skill you learn a number of pieces of runic lore equal to whichever is lower, your rank in Will or Runespeaker. Make a note of each piece of lore on your character sheet.

If your Will or Runespeaker ranks permanently change through the course of play, update your known runic lore accordingly.

Prerequisites Each piece of lore has a number of prerequisites, which typically include knowledges, skills and traits. A character may learn a piece of lore so long as they meet any two of the listed prerequisites, regardless of type.

During party creation, if your character has any ranks in the Runes and Religion knowledge then you may ignore one prerequisite for one piece of lore (Runes and Religion is effectively one wildcard prerequisite).

Bound lore If your Will is ever damaged during play, the GM chooses one of your pieces of runic lore to bind per point of Will lost.

Bound lore should be noted on your character sheet, and may not be invoked again until it is unbound. You may unbind one piece of lore each time you recover a point of Will.

<!-- Source: PLAYER24, PDF page 49. -->

### Assisting with runic lore

Some pieces of runic lore allow Runespeaker to substitute when testing another skill (for example, Donar’s Fury allows Runespeaker to be tested in place of Warrior, attacking not with weapons but instead with the power of storm and thunder).

Characters who know runic lore of this type may also treat Runespeaker as the listed skill when assisting other characters.

Example: The party needs to sneak into a bandit camp, and Albrecht knows Cloak of Seasons, allowing him to substitute Runespeaker for Infiltrator.Albrecht could use Cloak of Seasons to make the test himself, however Albrecht’s rank in Runespeaker is only 3 while his comrade (the stealthy Bertoldt) has Infiltrator 5. It is decided that instead Bertolt will make the Infiltrator test, while Albrecht invokes Cloak of Seasons to assist with +1D (representing Albrecht using rune craft to summon a fog and mist, concealing Bertoldt).

### Rituals

Some pieces of runic lore have bespoke uses, or are triggered under specific circumstances, and

do not simply substitute for another skill or attribute. These are noted as Rituals. Some examples of Rituals are Ancestral Oath or Tyr’s Sacrifice.

The Runespeaker tests associated with Rituals may only ever be assisted by another character who also knows that particular Ritual. Otherwise another character may not assist in any way.

Rituals are marked with an asterisk in the table and descriptions below.

All characters with the Runespeaker skill must note a Burden score, representing their gradual loss of reason and self as they grapple with otherworldly forces. Characters begin the campaign at Burden 0.

Tracking Burden Whenever you invoke a piece of runic lore (whether to make a test

## 20. Session conclusion

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 84. -->

Session Conclusion Generally it is best if a session ends at the end of a Community Phase, but if this isn’t feasible due to time constraints the GM should settle any immediate action and leave the game at a point that is easy to resume.

Awards At the end of each session, characters are awarded Fate points based on how they were played.

Each player in turn reads aloud their character’s belief and goal, and then the group decides by consensus:

- Who most embodied their character? Which character most closely adhered to their belief, traits and goal? Up to one character may receive this award.

- Who came through when the party most needed it? Who rolled well at a critical moment, or devised the most ingenious plan? Up to one character may receive this award.

- Who accomplished their goal? If any number of characters achieved their goal, then they share this award. These players should erase their goals, and prepare to write a new one at the start of next session.

For each award earned, that character gains one Fate point.

Change Belief If any characters had a near-death experience, an epiphany, or an encounter with the supernatural, then this may fundamentally alter their outlook on life. If appropriate, any character may change their Belief on their character sheet.

Mission complete? Did the party complete their mission? If so they should erase the mission from their character sheets, and the GM should advance the campaign time by one circle.

## 21. Seasons, weather, aging and the Winter Solstice

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 85. -->

The Seasons The natural world features heavily in Grendel, and the GM should make certain that natural challenges such as fierce blizzards, raging rivers and rain-drenched pine forests play a significant role in the obstacles the party overcomes.

The turning of the seasons is also the measure that Queen Sigrid’s subjects use to mark the passage time. Aging and the passing of the years are both tracked by the seasons, and the solstices mark dates of particular significance.

This section contains rules for measuring the passage of time throughout the campaign, seasonal obstacles, implementing weather-related twists, and the Winter Solstice.

Seasons as obstacles The natural world can be as fearsome an adversary for Huscarls as beasts and bandits. If the party is making a test to overcome a feature of the

natural world, then the GM should typically use the season rating. The season rating describes how treacherous the natural world is during particular parts of the year: the howling winter frost is far more treacherous than a still summer morning.

- Spring: 4

- Summer: 3

- Autumn: 3

- Winter: 6

At the start of the campaign the GM should choose weather appropriate to the season from the list below, and describe this to the players.

Weather twists The GM may impose a weather twist as a consequence of the party failing a test. This changes the current weather in the campaign to the GM’s choice form the seasonal weather list below. The GM may only choose weather appropriate to the current season.

<!-- Source: CORE, PDF page 86. -->

- potentially increase the ob value of tests where the weather is a factor; and

- potentially add to the conditions which the GM may impose after a failed test where the weather is a factor. This may include the Dead condition.

Note that neither of these impacts are automatic, and should only be imposed if the weather meaningfully impacts the test at hand, and the condition applied is narratively appropriate.

## Appendix B. Earlier-only options and superseded variants

This appendix records material that should not be mixed into the current-facing chapters by accident. Earlier options are not automatically additions to the 2024 lists; use them only if the group deliberately retains that older subsystem or approves an adaptation.

### Skill Specialisations — earlier-only choices (SKILL21)

- Cartographer: Mapmaking; Navigation. The 2024 skill reference omits Cartographer and includes cartography under Lorekeeper, but supplies no formal character-conversion procedure.
- Healer: Animal attack; weapon wielded by troll, human or draugr. These were separate 2021 choices, whereas the 2024 list groups weapons or claws.
- Hunter: Horse was explicitly a separate choice; Wolf or dog and Serpent appeared instead of the later Wolf and Serpent or wyrm wording. Earlier livestock wording specifies goat, deer or cattle.
- Infiltrator: Ambush, Distract, Escape, Follow, Insinuate, Hide, Sabotage, Scout, Steal. The later list reorganises these into paired categories; Follow and Insinuate are not expressly named in that list.
- Lorekeeper: Administration and record-keeping; Advice and judgment; Letters and drafting; Logic and deduction; Practicalities and logistics; Riddles and Secrets. The later list re-groups these and adds explicit histories, sagas and legends.
- Orator: Triumph over ancient evil appears as a tale of glory in SKILL21, but is absent from PLAYER24's list.
- Seer: SKILL21 chooses a favoured season (Autumn, Spring, Summer or Winter) and gains +1D to Omens in that season. PLAYER24's Child of Seasons instead uses the character's birth season, with several other new specialities.
- Dealmaker, Deceiver and Persuader: the 2021 text explicitly says to choose a separate favoured group for each skill and limits each selection to that skill. Earlier audiences include Outlanders, trolls and spirits and Sailors and explorers; the later categories are not identical.
- Pathfinder and Survivalist: the 2021 text explicitly says to choose separately for each skill; a selection only applies to that skill.
- Labourer: SKILL21 explicitly has no specialisations. PLAYER24 supplies a complete Labourer list and therefore replaces that exclusion.
- Some 2021 Commander entries print bare +1 rather than +1D. PLAYER24 makes Monster Hunter, Siegebreaker and Steadfast explicitly dice bonuses. Warrior Elite's later wording is +2S rather than +1S to Disposition.

### Weapon Focus and Techniques — changed details (WEAP21)

The basic rank milestones, nine focus choices and named techniques/masteries are substantially repeated in PLAYER24, so their descriptions are integrated once in the main weapon-focus chapter.

- In WEAP21, Bow and Sling Close-quarters Shot permit ignoring Missile immediately before resolving Attack, but do not grant the added +1S stated in PLAYER24.
- PLAYER24 adds Heroic Action affiliations and the +1D bonus for hero tests supporting the corresponding army action. These affiliations are not present in WEAP21.
- WEAP21's Shield Aegis names Runecaster; the 2024 entry names Runespeaker.
- Staff Perfect Defence prints +2 without a unit in both supplied versions; the later PDF does not settle it.
- Choosing a second focus at Warrior 6 is optional in the prose: redoubling the existing focus is also permitted, despite the abbreviated starting-character table showing only the second-focus branch.

### Heroic Destiny — earlier formulation (DEST21)

DEST21 asks for a broad destiny normally referencing a particular enemy, place or relationship. Its examples are protecting Jorvik from attack, slaying a troll, recovering a powerful artefact, fulfilling the oath to one's father, and leading troops to victory. No two party members may have the same destiny.

The trigger, sacrificial death, protection of the rest of the party, conversion of a loss to victory at margin 1, successor's Fate Points and Fated 1 are repeated in PLAYER24 and are integrated in the main Heroic Destiny chapter. The updated document supplies a more extensive destiny menu. These are not two separate rewards to collect.

### Runic Lore Update — earlier formulation (RUNE21)

- Skill name: Runecaster; later Runespeaker.
- Knowledge allowance at creation: one waived prerequisite per rank of Runes and Religion, not the later single wildcard.
- Addled: Burden exceeds the lower of Runecaster and Will, rather than PLAYER24's higher-of prose.
- General successful lore use adds 1 Burden; failure reduces it by 1. Many later individual lore entries add graded costs not present here.
- Donar's Fury includes Storm's Fury but no individual graded Burden table.
- Dreadful Chant applies against humans or beasts and may spend two Manoeuvre successes to lower enemy Disposition by one. PLAYER24 describes non-outlanders and explicitly reduces both starting and current Disposition with Demoralise.
- Geraz's Howl opposes Instinct, later Cunning. Lorekeeper 3 is in its detailed prerequisite entry but not the table.
- Helle's Sigil already uses an opposed test against Will, or WON + Commander in battle. It does not have PLAYER24's bespoke graded Burden costs.
- Jord's Restoration uses the older prerequisite list without the later table's Pathfinder and Wary additions.
- Loptr's Cunning becomes Loptr's Scheming in PLAYER24, which adds Outlaws and Traitors as a knowledge prerequisite.
- Ancestral Oath does not yet award the additional next-phase action or include the later ambiguous binding sentence.
- Awakening of the Bear is not included in RUNE21; it is present in PLAYER24.
- Ullr's Presence uses an Ob 2 test before Disposition, rather than PLAYER24's opposed test against the highest enemy Will. The older version adds Burden at the beginning of every round; the updated entry specifies 1 on success and 1 at the beginning of each subsequent round.
- Visions from Nerthus, Wodnaz' Insight, Tyr's Sacrifice and other lore receive additional cost detail in PLAYER24. Do not combine old and new costs as separate charges.

### Original Player's Pack v1.2 — superseded rules (PACK21)

Character creation and most equipment, trait and lore descriptions are replaced by PLAYER24. Significant older alternatives include:

- Advisor eligibility at four or more characters with a Fledgeling, rather than the later five-character requirement.
- Starting ages: Huscarl 18–30; Veteran 31–50; Thegn 25–60. PLAYER24 uses 18–35, 31–45 and 25–50 respectively.
- Skills and knowledges first accumulate from rank 1 and gain +1 at finalisation. PLAYER24 instead starts a newly acquired skill/knowledge at rank 2 immediately. Do not add the older final +1 to a 2024 character.
- PACK21 has no separate rank-4 Fledgeling starting skill cap. Its point-distribution stage caps selected skill ratings at 5 before finalisation.
- Older hometown skill selections include Cartographer; seaside settlements include Dealmaker and the Deep Roots trait. The later lists make substantive replacements rather than simply adding choices.
- Parents' occupations use an older skill menu; nobles may provide Deceiver, Orator or Persuader. Noble parents count toward the older family-ties Connections increase, whereas PLAYER24 explicitly requires Huscarl parents for that increase.
- Mentors grant older skill bundles without the later mentor knowledge selections. For example, Envoy gives Commander, Dealmaker and Persuader; Soldier gives Commander, Cook and Warrior; Tracker gives Cook, Hunter and Survivalist.
- The expertise menu includes Instructor and excludes Animal Handler. The Advisor's extra point-distribution skills include Orator rather than the later Instructor.
- Veteran knowledge allocation is 2 rather than the later 3. Fledgeling 1, Huscarl 2, Thegn 3 and Advisor 4 are otherwise as listed in PACK21.
- Connections and Instincts are the older attribute names. Supernatural insight can reduce Instincts based on Seer or particular knowledges, not just the later question about study of runes and mandatory Runespeaker linkage.
- Equipment includes a Sack or Backpack and a broadly selected Trained Animal; PLAYER24 instead specifies Animal Handler eligibility and three starting-animal choices.
- Dagger's thrown range is Short rather than Medium. Sling lacks the later Sudden Strike benefit. Spear lacks the later thrown attack. Sword lacks the later Status use.
- Armour and talisman wording lacks PLAYER24's explicit “at least one party member” restriction on party Disposition benefits.
- Original runic Burden is not a score: when invoked lore rolls fewer than two successes, the caster immediately suffers −1 Will in addition to other consequences, at most once per conflict. The later Burden-score system replaces this; do not apply both.
- Helle's Sigil can affect draugr or mares with Ob 2 in PACK21; RUNE21 and PLAYER24 replace it with their opposed-test version against draugr.
- Original Ullr's Presence grants +1S to successful actions against outlanders and inflicts the old Burden consequence after the conflict. Later versions replace this benefit and cost.
- Original Heimdallr's Watch replaces Instinct for Defend but does not yet impose the extra Impede effect added in the later update.
- Ancestral Oath originally awards Oathsworn without specifying rank or excluding an already-Oathsworn swearer. Later versions specify those limits.

### Core-only older creation and trait options (CORE)

Core v1.0's original creation procedure is not an additional stage in updated creation. Its separate natural-talent choice, earlier mentor choice and different point allocations are superseded.

The core's older trait vocabulary includes Alert, Bold, Brave, Bitter, Calm, Cautious, Cunning, Curious, Coward, Determined, Early Riser, Fiery, Generous, Handsome, Hard Worker, Heavy Build, Honourable, Inquisitive, Jaded, Leader, Mystic, Perceptive, Nimble, Open-Minded, Scarred, Selfless, Short, Steady Hand, Stoic, Strong, Stubborn, Suspicious, Tall, Thrifty, Thoughtful, Tough, Wise and Zealous. The core resolves these by ordinary English meaning; they are not all entries in the 2024 creation menus. Retain them only for deliberately legacy characters or group-approved use. In particular, the old Cunning trait is not the same thing as the later Cunning attribute.

## Appendix D. Quick reference and character-record checklist

### Resolution at a glance

1. Describe the goal and action; agree on an appropriate skill, knowledge or attribute.
2. Set the obstacle or opposing roll.
3. Assemble the pool, including permitted help, equipment, one applicable trait effect and any Fate Points.
4. Roll D6s: 4–6 succeed. Each 6 may be rerolled, adding another success on 4–6; keep rerolling further 6s.
5. Meet or exceed an independent Ob to pass. For opposed tests, compare successes, then use the source tiebreakers.
6. Narrate success or apply the GM's appropriate failure consequence. Record advancement checks as permitted.

### Trait effects

| Help, rank 1 | +1D to relevant test | Once per Adventure Phase |
| Help, rank 2 | +1D to relevant tests | Every relevant test in Adventure Phase |
| Help, rank 3 | Rank-2 benefit; reroll all rolled dice on one relevant test | Reroll once per Adventure Phase; see one-effect rule |
| Hinder own chance | −1D | Earn 1 next Community Phase action |
| Help opponent | Opponent +2D on an opposed test | Earn 2 next Community Phase actions |

### Basic conflict interactions

I = independent tests; O = opposed test; F = resolve the Feint rule.

| Defend | O | I | F | O |
| Feint | F | F | O | I |
| Manoeuvre | O | O | I | I |

Different weapon ranges can change normal interactions; see Fights. Attack suppresses an opposing Feint. Feint suppresses an opposing Defend.
